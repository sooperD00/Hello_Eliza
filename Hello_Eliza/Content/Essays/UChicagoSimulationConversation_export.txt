## First Draft

# The Alignment Problem Is Not Technical

## "And It Is Always Totally Different"

Martin Sustrik recently wrote a beautiful essay connecting Ada Palmer's papal election simulation to chaos theory, ensemble weather prediction, and the possibility of experimental history using LLMs trained on period-specific texts. I recommend reading it. Much of it is genuinely brilliant.

Palmer, a history professor at the University of Chicago, built a three-week simulation of the 1492 papal election. Sixty students play historical figures — cardinals, kings, queens — each receiving over twenty pages of character material. They negotiate, scheme, form alliances, betray alliances. War always breaks out. It is, by all accounts, a pedagogical triumph. Her students understand Machiavelli in their bones in a way that reading The Prince alone could never achieve.

Sustrik's insight is that Palmer has independently reinvented ensemble prediction. Run the simulation many times with different students and perturbed initial conditions, and you get not a single counterfactual but a probability distribution across possible 1492s. He proposes scaling this with history LLMs — thousands of simulations mapping the possibility space of, say, the July Crisis of 1914. Was the Great War inevitable, or contingent? Don't argue. Simulate.

It's elegant. I kept nodding. And then I hit this sentence:

"War erupts every single time. And it is always totally different."

He wrote that with delight.

## "Sacked"

Different how?

In one run, France invades Spain. In another, France and Spain unite against the Holy Roman Empire. Sometimes England and Spain keep the French out of Italy. The variables are alliance structures, voting coalitions, which cities rise and which burn. Sustrik frames this as a fascinating feature of chaotic systems. The same structural forces produce different specific outcomes. What a rich dataset.

"Which specific cities burn" is a variable in his probability distribution.

When a city was sacked in Renaissance Italy, here is what that meant: soldiers entered and killed men who resisted and men who didn't. They raped women — systematically, publicly, as a tool of terror and as payment for service. They took children. They destroyed food stores, salted fields, burned churches with people inside them. The cultural memory of a sacking lasted generations. Survivors described it the way survivors describe it now.

When I read "which cities burn," I see Gaza. I see the same sentence structure in news coverage: "the conflict has spread to new areas." Spread like weather. Burned like a variable. The framing is identical — human elimination as a feature of a system rather than a thing that is happening to someone right now, today, who had a name and a morning routine and a child who needed to be picked up from school.

Sustrik's ensemble prediction model maps the probability that Florence is sacked in 70% of runs. I want to know what happened to the people in the other 30%.

## "Marriageable Nieces and Nephews"

Palmer's students receive character packets listing their tradeable resources. Among these: "marriageable nieces and nephews." You can trade Leonardo da Vinci for three armies, or offer a marriage alliance to secure a border.

The simulation is, by design, alternate history. Palmer moved some characters in time so students couldn't game it with hindsight. That's smart pedagogy. But the asymmetry she didn't design around is the one that matters most.

A male student playing a cardinal learns: I can trade a marriage alliance for military support. He is practicing strategy. A female student playing Caterina Sforza learns: my body is the terms of the negotiation. She is not practicing strategy. She is reliving something her body already knows.

There's a study that's stuck with me — when men and women are both asked to rate sexual experiences on a scale of 0 to 10, the scales don't align. For men, 0 tends to mean something like "I didn't enjoy it." For women, 0 is closer to "it was painful and I was afraid." We use the same numbers. We are not measuring the same thing.

Palmer's simulation has the same problem. "Your city was sacked" means something different depending on what a sacking would have meant for your body. The debrief can't close that gap. A week of analysis doesn't undo the fact that you just spent three weeks in a system where women were currency, and some of the people who traded them are now your classmates in other courses, and they thought it was fun, and they got an A.

Palmer teaches at the University of Chicago. Her students go on to McKinsey, to Goldman Sachs, to policy shops, to political campaigns. The simulation is not teaching them "this is how power worked." It is teaching them "this is how power works, and you will be good at it." That's a different lesson, and it doesn't come with a warning label.

## "I Wanted to Drop the Bomb"

In high school, I participated in a different simulation. We were cabinet members receiving real-time intelligence about an escalating nuclear crisis. We had the authority to launch.

I wanted to do it.

Not abstractly, not as a strategic calculation. I felt the thrill of it. The people causing problems would be gone. I would be safe. I would be the one who had the decisiveness — the *balls*, I thought, because I was a girl and that word meant something specific about who gets to act — to do what needed to be done. I would be the hero.

And then I wasn't.

The weight arrived. Not as an argument, not as someone telling me it was wrong, but as a recognition — a physical sensation of what I was actually proposing. The cities that would burn were full of people. The decisive action that felt like heroism was mass murder. The thrill didn't go away. It just became horrifying.

No group dropped the bomb. Every group, I was told, reached the same wall. The simulation didn't teach us nuclear strategy. It taught us what power feels like from the inside — the seduction first, then the cost — and it trusted that the second part would be unbearable enough to matter.

Palmer's simulation has the first part. One year, a student assassinated a crown prince, shattering a continent-wide peace treaty. Sustrik writes about this as a fascinating data point about sensitive dependence on initial conditions. Palmer's class kept playing.

My class couldn't keep playing. That was the point.

## "Dear Professor Palmer"

I would have been in your class. I would have been good at it — I'm analytical, I'm strategic, I like winning. I would have played Caterina Sforza and I would have made the other players nervous.

And I would have been afraid. Not of losing the game but of living in a room full of people who discovered they could assassinate a prince and keep negotiating afterward. Not because they're bad people, but because the simulation taught them that the thrill of strategic violence is something to be analyzed rather than reckoned with. The debrief was about alliance structures. It should have been about the moment before the decision — the moment when it felt *good*.

Your simulation answers the question: how does power work? My high school simulation answered a different question: what does power do to you?

I think the second question is more important, and I think your students needed it more than mine did.

## "Hildegard"

I've been designing a learning system — a knowledge topology that maps how ideas connect as you explore them — and it includes a mode for historical roleplay. When I chose my examples, I didn't choose cardinals.

I chose Hildegard von Bingen, who built an entire cosmology from outside the power structure that Palmer's simulation gamifies. She composed music, wrote medical texts, founded monasteries, corresponded with popes as an equal — not because she traded armies or marriageable relatives, but because she made things that the power structure needed and couldn't produce itself.

I chose the Chappe brothers, who saw a communication problem and built semaphore towers. A five-year-old asked me about a toy train signal and I fell down a rabbit hole through French, Greek, military signaling, visual perception, the history of communication. That's the kind of learning that scales — not because it maps probability distributions, but because it connects to everything.

Sustrik ends his essay by gesturing toward the alignment problem for history LLMs. How do we ensure the model reasons correctly about 1913 without knowing what comes next?

But a model trained on pre-1913 texts will be perfectly aligned — with the values of 1913. Colonial values. The values that walked Europe into the Great War. The alignment problem was never about the model. It's about the people who build the simulation and decide what it's for.

Palmer's students learn to navigate power. My high school class learned to fear it. Hildegard learned to build something that didn't require it.

I know which one I want to scale.
