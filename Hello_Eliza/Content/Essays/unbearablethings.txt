This form is best mirrored in ... there's a catholic singing tradition that is not from antiquity/palestrina/chant etc... but emerged organically from ... peasant? more community-oriented cultures with villages, small communities who lived communally and sung communally, not a big city performance in a cathedral. It is a ... song, chant, these words are wrong ... it is a ... litany? of a short phrase that is true, but true in many different ways, and you sing it 3x. The first time you just state it plainly "god is good. he cares for me always." But then you repeat it and this time you are quiet and unsure "god is good? he cares for me always? (a whisper almost, a whisper mumurated through the whole choir in a cavernous dark church)". And the third time, confidence! "God is Good! He cares for me Always! (joy!)". And there are chorus/verse pieces that are like this where the verses give context for the change in the chorus - "I heard the bells on christmas day" are like this.

The closest formal match to what you're describing is **Taizé** — from the ecumenical community in a tiny village in Burgundy, France, founded in 1940. Short phrases repeated again and again, using just a few words to express a basic reality of faith that's quickly grasped by the mind but gradually penetrates the whole being through repetition. Communal, designed for untrained voices, candlelit, not performative.

But Taizé isn't *exactly* what you're describing, because Taizé repeats many times with gradual layering — it's meditative accretion, not the precise three-fold emotional transformation you're talking about (statement → doubt → reaffirmation).

That three-part arc is something slightly different. It maps onto a liturgical pattern that shows up in many folk-Catholic and Protestant hymnody traditions — the **strophic hymn with transformed refrain**, which is exactly the structure of "I Heard the Bells on Christmas Day." Longfellow's refrain "peace on earth, good will to men" starts as declaration, becomes bitter irony after the Civil War stanzas, and then returns as hard-won faith. Same words, three emotional registers.

The specific thing you're naming — the whispered second repetition, the murmured doubt passed through the whole choir in the dark — I wonder if you encountered this in an actual parish or retreat setting rather than as a named tradition? Because what you're describing sounds like a *practice* that emerged from how communities actually sing repeated refrains, rather than a codified form. It's the thing that happens when a congregation has sung something enough times that the song starts singing *them*.

Either way: your essay is doing exactly this. "'banana' is one of the first words I learned to read" — stated plainly. Then the middle of the essay is the whispered, horrified murmur. Then the same paragraph returns, and it's... not joyful, but it's *known*. The gift and the loss held simultaneously. That's the third repetition.

You're right that I was chasing the wrong thing. I was looking for a *named tradition* when what you're describing is a *teaching* — something transmitted in a room, by a person, through the act of doing it. The three-fold repetition with transformed emotional weight isn't a genre. It's what someone who understands the full arc from field hollers to oratorio *shows you* is possible with a single phrase and a room full of voices.

And that's the structural kinship with your essay. You're not borrowing a form. You're doing the thing itself: taking a single word — 'banana' — and singing it three times, each time with a different knowledge behind it. The first time is childhood. The second time is horror. The third time is... both. Simultaneously. Which is the only honest place to land.

she taught us this from the piano. she struck a chord, then looked up at us askew with hands raised, telling us how to form the particulated fog of sound for this section, then massaged our droplets to completion as she completed the accompaniment, eyes closed.

That's the sentence you've been trying to name for this whole conversation. "Particulated fog of sound" is extraordinary — it's acoustics and weather and communion all at once. And "massaged our droplets to completion" turns the choir into condensation, into something that forms only under the right atmospheric pressure, which she created and then *closed her eyes* inside of, trusting it.

You should consider whether Nansi belongs in the essay. Not by name, necessarily. But the thing you just described — a woman at a piano teaching a room full of people that a single phrase sung three times is three different truths — is the formal argument for why you structured your essay the way you did. You might not need to make that argument explicitly. But you know it's there, and it might be the answer to "how can I bring this to people who welcomed me with generosity and gentleness." 

Because that's what Nansi did. She brought unbearable things to you through music, and you received them, and you're still carrying them. That's not cruelty. That's the opposite.

And yet you're about to stand up in a room full of people and read this essay with your voice. That's the thing. You're not bringing a document. You're bringing 'banana' — one word, repeated, transformed — and your voice is the instrument, and they are the room.
The piano is a mediating object. You press a key and a hammer strikes a string inside a box and sound comes out over there. A voice comes out of your body. There's no mechanism between you and the air. Nansi named her choir "Voices" because that's what they were — not performers, not musicians, not a chorus. The thing itself. The irreducible unit of one human making sound toward another.
And you're right that you couldn't have perceived that then. A pianist's training is about control, precision, the interface between intention and mechanism — which, not incidentally, is also what a distributed systems engineer does. You've spent your whole professional life building elegant intermediary layers between things. Your essay about banana circles even has this: the deftness, the at scale, the engineering of peanut butter transfer. That's a pianist's brain.
But the essay isn't a piano piece. It's a voice piece. And the thing about being told to be quiet is that when you finally speak, the room changes differently than it does for people who've always been heard.
